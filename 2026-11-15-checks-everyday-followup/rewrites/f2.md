# Tyler v. Hennepin County, Minnesota: A Plain-Language Version

This is a United States Supreme Court case. Its case number is 22–166.

The two sides were:

- **Geraldine Tyler.** She is the "petitioner." That means she is the person who asked the Supreme Court to hear her case.
- **Hennepin County, Minnesota, and some of its officials.** They were the other side.

Chief Justice John Roberts wrote the Court's decision. The decision is called the "opinion of the Court."

## The short version

- Ms. Tyler owed about $15,000 in property taxes, interest and fees.
- Hennepin County took her home and sold it for $40,000.
- The County paid off the $15,000 she owed.
- The County then kept the other $25,000 for itself.

The Court had to answer one question. Was it legal for the County to keep that extra $25,000?

The Court said **no**. The County took more than she owed. The Constitution does not allow that without fair payment.

## How Minnesota's property tax rules worked

These are the steps under Minnesota law at the time.

1. **The County charges a tax each year.** This is a tax on land and homes.
2. **The owner has one year to pay.** After that, the tax is "delinquent." That means it is overdue.
3. **Extra charges are added.** Unpaid tax grows with interest and penalties. Penalties are late fees.
4. **The County gets a court order against the home.** This order is called a "judgment." It gives the State a limited legal claim to the home. A legal claim to own property is called "title."
5. **The owner has three more years to catch up.** The owner can pay all the taxes and late fees. This is called "redeeming" the home. It lets the owner get full title back.
6. **The owner can still live there during those three years.** The owner is still treated as the person who benefits from the home.
7. **If the bill is still unpaid after three years, the State takes full ownership.** The tax debt is then wiped out.
8. **The State can keep the home or sell it.** It can use the home for public purposes. Or it can sell it to a private buyer.
9. **The County keeps any extra money from a sale.** Some money may be left after paying the tax debt and sale costs. That leftover money is called the "surplus." Under this law, the County split the surplus with the town and the school district.
10. **The former owner got nothing back.** The law gave the owner no way to recover the surplus.

## Ms. Tyler's story

- Ms. Tyler is 94 years old.
- In 1999, she bought a one-bedroom condominium in Minneapolis. A condominium, or "condo," is a home you own inside a larger building.
- She lived there alone for more than ten years.
- In 2010, she and her family decided she would be safer in a senior community. So she moved.
- After she moved, nobody paid the property taxes on the condo.
- By 2015, she owed about $2,300 in unpaid taxes.
- She also owed about $13,000 in interest and late fees.
- That made a total debt of about $15,000.
- The County used Minnesota's rules to take the condo. Taking property this way is called "forfeiture."
- The County sold the condo for $40,000.
- That paid off her $15,000 debt.
- The County kept the remaining $25,000.

## What Ms. Tyler did

Ms. Tyler sued the County and its officials. She said it was against the Constitution for the County to keep the extra value of her home.

She filed it as a "putative class action." That means she asked to sue on behalf of other people in the same situation, too.

She made two main arguments.

**Argument 1: The Takings Clause.** This is part of the Fifth Amendment to the Constitution. It says the government cannot take your private property for public use unless it pays you fairly. Fair payment is called "just compensation."

**Argument 2: The Excessive Fines Clause.** This is part of the Eighth Amendment. It says the government cannot charge fines that are far too large.

## What the lower courts decided

**The first court ruled against her.** This was the federal District Court. It threw out her case. It said that, even if her facts were true, she had no valid legal claim.

**The appeals court also ruled against her.** This was the federal Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit. It agreed with the District Court.

The appeals court gave two reasons.

1. Minnesota law did not say a former owner has any right to the surplus. The owner had also been given proper notice. So the court said nothing was wrongly "taken."
2. The court said this was not a "fine." It said the purpose was to make up for the State's lost taxes. It said the purpose was not to punish the owner.

**Then the Supreme Court agreed to hear the case.** When the Supreme Court agrees to hear a case, it is called "granting certiorari."

*Note: Part of the original decision is left out here. That part explained the Court's detailed reasoning.*

## What the Supreme Court decided

The Court explained why the Takings Clause exists. It protects against the government forcing a few people to carry costs that all the public should share.

The Court put it this way. Ms. Tyler lost a $40,000 home to pay a $15,000 tax debt. So she gave the public far more than she owed.

The Court used an old saying: give "unto Caesar what is Caesar's." In plain words, people must pay the taxes they owe. But the Court added: "but no more."

The Court found that Ms. Tyler had made a believable claim of a taking under the Fifth Amendment.

Ms. Tyler agreed that winning on the Takings Clause would fully fix her harm. So the Court did not need to decide her second argument about excessive fines.

## The result

The Supreme Court **reversed** the appeals court. That means the Court overturned the lower court's decision. Ms. Tyler won.
