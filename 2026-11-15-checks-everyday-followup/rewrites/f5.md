# 2026 Poverty Guidelines: A Plain-Language Guide

These are the yearly income limits from the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services (HHS). HHS is the federal agency that runs health and family programs.

## What this notice does

HHS updates the "poverty guidelines" every year. The poverty guidelines are income amounts. Many aid programs use them to decide who can get help.

This year's update raises the amounts to keep up with rising prices. Prices went up 2.63 percent from 2024 to 2025.

## When the new amounts start

The new amounts start on **January 13, 2026**. A program can choose a different start date. Ask your program if you are not sure.

## Who uses these guidelines

Medicaid uses them. Medicaid is a government health insurance program for people with low income. Many other programs use them too.

A law requires HHS to update the guidelines at least once a year. The law is 42 U.S.C. 9902(2).

## How HHS sets the amounts

The Census Bureau is the government office that counts people. It publishes its own poverty numbers. HHS starts with those numbers.

Then HHS raises them for higher prices. It uses a price measure called the Consumer Price Index for All Urban Consumers (CPI-U). The CPI-U tracks how much everyday goods cost in cities.

Then HHS rounds the numbers. It also sets an equal step between each family size.

Sometimes rounding makes an amount go down a little, even when prices rose. When that happens, HHS keeps last year's amount instead.

## How to read the tables

- Each amount is **yearly income**, before you look at any program's rules.
- Find the number of people in your family or household.
- Read the amount next to it.
- Use the table for where you live.

## Why Alaska and Hawaii are different

Alaska and Hawaii have their own, higher amounts. This practice began in the late 1960s.

## Puerto Rico and U.S. territories

There are no separate amounts for Puerto Rico or other U.S. territories. Each program decides what to use there. Ask your program office.

## Programs may use a percent of the guidelines

Some programs do not use the exact amounts. They use a percent of them. For example, a program might use 125 percent or 185 percent.

**Example:** For a family of 4 in the 48 states, 185 percent is $33,000 × 1.85. That equals $61,050.

Ask your program which percent it uses.

## What the guidelines do not cover

The amounts are the same for:
- Farm families and non-farm families.
- Older people and younger people.

This notice does **not** define "income" or "family." Each program has its own meaning for these words.

Ask the program that runs or pays for your aid about questions like:
- Do you count income before taxes (gross) or after taxes (net)?
- What income do you count, and what do you leave out?
- Who counts as part of my household?

## A note on the name

People sometimes call these the "OMB poverty guidelines." OMB means the Office of Management and Budget. This name is a mistake from old wording in a 1972 law. HHS issues the guidelines, not OMB.
