# Who Can Take FMLA Leave (§ 825.110)

The Family and Medical Leave Act (FMLA) is a federal law. It lets some workers take time off for family or medical reasons without losing their job. This rule explains who can use it. A worker who can use it is called an "eligible employee."

## (a) The three basic tests

You are an eligible employee if your employer is covered by FMLA and you meet all three of these tests:

1. **Time on the job.** You have worked for this employer for at least 12 months.
2. **Hours worked.** You have worked at least 1,250 hours in the 12 months right before your leave starts. That is about 24 hours a week. (Airline flight crews have their own hours rule in § 825.801.)
3. **Company size near you.** Your employer has at least 50 workers within 75 miles of where you work. (§ 825.105(b) covers workers outside the U.S.)

## (b) Counting your 12 months

Your 12 months do not have to be in a row. Time from different stretches with the same employer can add up.

**Long breaks.** A "break in service" means a time when you did not work for this employer. If your break was seven years or more, your work from before the break usually does not count.

**Exceptions.** Your work from before a break of more than seven years still counts in these cases:

- **Military service.** The break was because of military duty. This duty is protected by the Uniformed Services Employment and Reemployment Rights Act (USERRA). USERRA is a law that protects the jobs of people who serve in the military. Your time away for that service also counts toward your 12 months. But this rule does not give you more than USERRA already gives you.
- **Written promise to rehire.** You and your employer had a written agreement that you would be hired back. A union contract counts. For example, you may have left to go to school or to raise a child.

**What counts as a week.** A week counts as a week of work if you were on the payroll for any part of it. This includes paid or unpaid leave, like sick days or vacation. It also includes weeks when the employer gave you other benefits, like workers' comp or health insurance. If you work off and on, 52 weeks of work equals 12 months.

**Employers can choose to count more.** An employer may decide to count work from before a break of more than seven years. If it does, it must treat everyone with a similar break the same way.

## (c) Counting your 1,250 hours

Your hours are counted using the rules of the Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA). The FLSA is the federal law about pay and hours of work. (See 29 CFR part 785.) Airline flight crews have a different rule in § 825.801.

What matters is the hours you actually worked. It does not matter how the employer kept records. It does not matter if your pay deal leaves out some of your real hours. Any true record of the hours you actually worked can be used.

## (d) When the 12 months and 1,250 hours are checked

Both tests are checked on the day your FMLA leave is supposed to start.

You might already be on leave that is not FMLA leave when you reach your 12 months. If so, any part of that leave after you reach 12 months can become FMLA leave. This applies if the reason for the leave is one FMLA covers. (§ 825.300(b) explains what your employer must tell you about whether you qualify.)

## (e) When the 50-worker test is checked

The 50-workers-within-75-miles test is checked when you tell your employer you need leave.

Once you are found eligible, later changes in staff do not take that away. This is true for that one request for leave. It does not matter if you take the leave all at once or in pieces. It also does not matter if you take it as a shorter work schedule.

Your employer also cannot end leave that has already started because the worker count drops below 50.

**Example:** In August, your employer has 60 workers. The employer expects to have only 40 workers by December. In August, you ask for leave that will start in December. You meet all the other tests. Your employer must give you the FMLA leave.
