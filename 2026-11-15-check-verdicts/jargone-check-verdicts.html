jargone's seven checks: what the tests found

Every test of every check in one place: four kinds of text, two rounds of fixes, and about $5 of model calls since 10 November. For each check, what it does, what the tests showed, what to do with it and why.

What was tested

Kind of textWhat it wasWhen
Software documents3 documents from software projects: a 5,000-word review summary, a handover note and a plugin README31 Oct

How a finding was judged. A model played the named reader and said whether it would understand each flagged word. From 15 November it had to explain the word, and a second model marked the explanation right or wrong. For J07 it said whether the plainer word fits; for J05, whether the item was lost or only written another way. Sentence breaks for J03 were checked by hand. Every test since 10 November wrote down its rule before its results.

Bars show the share of findings that were right. The line marks 50%, the pass mark the tests used.
J01

Words used before they are explained

Drop

It looks up each word in a list of how familiar 59,366 English words are. A word below the cut-off that is used before the text explains it gets flagged, as a word this reader may not know.

Why drop it. On every kind of text it was wrong far more often than right. Most false alarms were words the reader already knew, or names. It also misses most of what readers don't know: in the government texts the reader named 192 terms it would not understand, and J01 and J04 together flagged 30. In the trial, false alarms from the word-list checks pushed the writer into worse text three times.

What replaces it. A model reading as the named reader judges each term. The code that finds the words a person uses, and the terms a text explains, stays: the calibration design reuses it.

J02

One thing, one name

Keep

If your glossary says two words mean the same thing, for example "job" and "task", it flags a text that uses both, so the reader is not left wondering whether they differ.

Why keep it. No test ran it, because it only acts on pairs you declare, so it cannot raise false alarms on its own. It costs nothing when no glossary exists.

Still to learn. Whether people use glossaries at all. Test it the first time one is used in earnest.

J03

Long sentences

Keep, fix first

It flags a sentence over 30 words, or over 20 inside a numbered list of steps.

Kind of textWhat happened
Software documentsEarned its place in the trial, with no separate count.
Software-session replies35 flags, 18 right (51%). False alarms: numbered lists held to the 20-word limit for steps, and sentences joined by mistake.
US government textsMissed 3 long sentences in the Medicare rule (43, 46 and 36 words), because it ended sentences at "Pub." as in "Pub. 100-01". Made 20 false breaks in the court opinion at citations such as "Minn. Stat.", none of which changed a flag.
Long-form articles145 flags. It missed 17 sentence ends where the full stop sits inside a closing quotation mark (…first-gen." While…): 3 false flags, 1 flag hidden, 3 wrong lengths.

Why keep it. Long sentences are a real difficulty for readers, and on documents it is right about most of them. Its errors come from deciding where sentences end, which is fixable: legal abbreviations (JAR-003) and full stops inside quotation marks (JAR-005). Both fixes are due now.

For replies. Off by default and available as an option, since half its findings on replies were false alarms.

Why drop it. Wherever there were enough findings to judge, none of its 35 was right. It shares J01's weakness: a word list cannot tell what this reader knows. A model reading as the named reader can judge an opening as a whole.

J05

Nothing lost

Keep, fix first

It checks that every number, piece of code and link target in the original is still in the rewrite, or in the reference file the detail moved to. It is /jargone's core check.

Kind of textFindingsReal lossesShare right
Software documentsEarned its place in the trial, with no separate count.
US government texts, before the fix7554

US government texts, after the fix6451

All on one-shot rewrites. The first fix (JAR-004) taught it that a date, a thousands separator and paragraph numbering can be written another way. Long-form articles hold few numbers and no citations, so it had little to check there.

Why keep it. It catches losses a machine can check exactly: 51 real ones in the government rewrites, mostly citations a plain rewrite dropped. Its remaining false alarms are of known kinds:

  • A number still there, used fewer times: 11 of 13 on government texts and 5 of 9 on long-form. Whether these stay errors or become warnings is your choice. As warnings, its errors on government texts would be right 96% of the time.
  • Numbers written as words (14 as "fourteen") and decades ("the '70s" as "the 1970s"): 4 on long-form, filed as JAR-006.
J06

Readability scores

Keep

It reports the Flesch reading ease, a school grade level, and the average and longest sentence. The scores are for information and never pass or fail a text.

Why keep it. It costs nothing and blocks nothing. Its formulas are rough, as jargone's specification says. It also inherits J03's sentence counting: in the court opinion, 20 false sentence breaks made the average sentence look shorter than it is. The J03 fixes improve it too.

Another tool on the same texts

To see whether these results are particular to jargone, the text audit from the open-source iso-24495 plugin was run on the same five government texts and judged the same way.

  • The same pattern holds. Its acronym rule was mostly wrong about what the reader would not understand, as J01 was. Its swap list worked on formal government writing, as J07's did. Its sentence counting worked, with the same weakness at abbreviations such as "Pub.".
  • It claims no more than this. It calls its findings "mechanical proxies, not an ISO judgement", and that holds. The failure is in using word lists to judge what a particular reader understands, which no list can see.
  • The checks that work here are already in a free tool. jargone does not need to rebuild long-sentence and plainer-word checks to offer them. Its one check with no counterpart there is J05, nothing lost.

Full results: the iso-24495 audit test. Cost $0.45.

What these tests cannot tell us

  • The reader is a model. It cannot forget what it knows. On the long-form articles it explained 98 of 100 flagged terms, including science words like "nucleobases" that a real curious adult may not know. The verdicts on J01 and J04 rest on this reader.
  • The samples are small. Five texts per kind, one reader each, one judgment per finding. J04 and J07 had few findings outside the long-form test.
  • J07's two lists are different lists. The old list's failures and the trimmed list's successes do not describe one list.

  2. Build the due fixes in one change. J03's sentence counting (JAR-003, JAR-005), J05's numbers in words and decades (JAR-006), and your J05 choice. Each comes with a test that fails without it.
